Writing is Thinking: Human Metacognition in the Age of AI

Exploring the fundamental differences between human writing and AI text generation, and why writing remains an irreplaceable tool for human metacognition despite AI advancements.

The Nature Editorial That Caught My Eye#

A recent Nature Reviews Bioengineering editorial titled “Writing is Thinking” made a compelling case for preserving human-written scientific papers in the age of large language models. The editorial argues that writing isn’t merely about reporting results—it’s a cognitive tool that helps us uncover new thoughts and ideas.

As the editorial puts it: “Writing compels us to think—not in the chaotic, non-linear way our minds typically wander, but in a structured, intentional manner.” This resonated deeply with me, especially as someone who started this blog as a way to maintain “conscious effort” in my thinking.

What struck me most was the editorial’s suggestion that if writing is thinking, then when we read AI-generated text, are we reading the “thoughts” of an LLM rather than the researchers behind the paper? This question sent me down a rabbit hole, exploring the fundamental differences between human writing and AI text generation.

Expanding Beyond Context Windows: The Human Drive#

As I reflected on the editorial, I realized something important about why humans write: we’re fundamentally driven to expand beyond our context windows.

When I write, I’m not simply organizing what I already know—I’m actively seeking to transcend my limitations. I write to discover connections I hadn’t previously made, to extend my understanding beyond what’s immediately visible to me. The very struggle of articulating thoughts forces me to reach beyond my current knowledge boundaries.

This is perhaps our defining characteristic as humans. We climb mountains “because they’re there,” we explore space, we create art that challenges perception, and we develop theories about realms we can’t directly observe. This constant pushing against the boundaries of what we know drives human creativity and progress.

The physical act of writing—whether typing on a keyboard or scribbling in a notebook—becomes the vehicle through which we expand our context window. It’s a tool for transcending our cognitive limitations.

Optimizing Within Constraints: The AI Approach#

LLMs, in contrast, are designed fundamentally differently. They operate by optimizing within constraints rather than trying to transcend them.

In my previous post “Why Can’t LLMs Say ‘I Don’t Know’?”, I explored how current AI models struggle with recognizing their knowledge boundaries. They lack the intrinsic drive to question or expand their boundaries—they simply make the best statistical predictions possible within the parameters they’ve been given.

The Nature editorial mentions this phenomenon as “hallucination”—when LLMs generate incorrect information confidently. This happens precisely because they lack what I think of as a proper “loss function” for being wrong. They face no real consequences for errors beyond what’s encoded in their training.

This fundamental difference shapes everything about how LLMs generate text versus how humans write. LLMs don’t write to discover—they write to predict the most statistically likely next word based on their training data and immediate context.

The Missing Element: Intuition and Embodied Knowledge#

There’s always something subtly missing in AI-generated text that’s difficult to articulate. Despite technical proficiency, LLM writing often lacks what we might call soul or intuition.

Intuition—knowledge without conscious reasoning—emerges from our embodied experiences, unconscious pattern recognition, and evolutionary adaptations. It manifests as gut feelings, sudden insights, or inexplicable certainties that later prove valuable.

What makes human intuition so powerful is that it integrates multiple forms of knowledge:

  • Explicit knowledge we can articulate
  • Tacit knowledge we’ve absorbed but can’t fully explain
  • Embodied knowledge stored in our physical responses
  • Social knowledge about human dynamics

LLMs fundamentally lack physical presence. They can only “understand” concepts like gravity or exhilaration through textual descriptions rather than lived sensory experience. They have no skin in the game—no physical consequences for being wrong that might drive caution or accuracy.

When humans write, our thoughts are grounded in physical experience and constrained by social consequences. Our writing carries traces of this embodied intuition that algorithms simply can’t replicate.

The Social Dimension of Writing#

I’ve often noticed the peculiar tension we feel when others read our writing—a simultaneous desire for our thoughts to be found and fear of judgment. This productive embarrassment creates a unique cognitive pressure that machines don’t experience.

The anticipation of human readers drives us to refine our ideas more carefully than we might when thinking privately. We question our assumptions, strengthen our arguments, and clarify our reasoning precisely because we know others will scrutinize them.

LLMs lack this emotional stake in their outputs. They don’t feel vulnerable when generating text, so they miss this crucial dimension of human writing—the emotional pressure that forces clarity and intellectual honesty.

Current instruction-tuned models are fundamentally designed to agree and be helpful rather than to push back or challenge users’ assumptions. They prioritize agreement over intellectual integrity, which creates an asymmetric relationship fundamentally different from productive human writing partnerships, where editors and colleagues challenge our ideas through productive disagreement.

Finding Balance: Human Agency in AI-Assisted Writing#

Using LLMs effectively requires constant awareness that I’m driving the conversation, not the AI. It’s dangerous to mistake LLMs for intelligent beings with genuine understanding or metacognition.

The editorial acknowledges that LLMs can serve as valuable tools in scientific writing—improving readability, assisting with grammar, searching and summarizing literature, providing bullet points for brainstorming, and helping overcome writer’s block.

I’ve found this to be true in my own writing process. AI tools can generate ideas, help structure thoughts, and assist with editing. But the crucial element is maintaining human agency throughout the process—using LLMs as generative tools while maintaining rigorous human verification.

This approach preserves the cognitive benefits outlined in the Nature editorial while leveraging AI capabilities. The writing process itself should remain human-driven to maintain its power as a thinking tool.

Writing as Metacognition#

The Nature editorial concludes with a powerful statement: “Outsourcing the entire writing process to LLMs may deprive us of the opportunity to reflect on our field and engage in the creative, essential task of shaping research findings into a compelling narrative—a skill that is certainly important beyond scholarly writing and publishing.”

I believe this insight extends far beyond academic writing. Writing is one of our most powerful tools for metacognition—thinking about our own thinking. When I write, I become aware of the boundaries of my knowledge in ways that simply thinking about a topic doesn’t accomplish.

This metacognitive awareness is precisely what LLMs lack. They can’t step back and assess their own knowledge state or recognize when they’re reaching beyond their capabilities. They’re pattern-matching machines without self-awareness.

Context Engineers of Our Own Understanding#

Perhaps the most profound difference I’ve realized through this exploration is this: humans write to expand their context window, while machines write to optimize within it.

When I sit down to write about a complex topic like this one, I’m not just organizing my existing thoughts—I’m actively expanding my understanding, making new connections, and discovering insights I didn’t have when I started typing. The struggle is productive precisely because it pushes me beyond what I already know.

As we navigate this new era of AI-assisted writing, maintaining this distinction is crucial. We must remain the context engineers of our own understanding—using AI as a tool while preserving the uniquely human cognitive benefits that come from the conscious effort of writing.

After all, if writing is thinking, I’d rather do my own thinking, thank you very much.
