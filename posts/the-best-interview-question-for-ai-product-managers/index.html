The Best Interview Question for AI Product Managers

Why asking 'Show me your best prompt' reveals everything about an AI product manager's competency - and how to evaluate their answers.

I was reading a fascinating piece on context engineering today1 when it hit me: we’ve been overthinking AI product manager interviews. Instead of complex case studies or theoretical questions, there’s one simple ask that reveals everything: “Show me your best prompt.”

It’s like asking a developer to show their cleanest code. In a single example, you can assess their understanding of AI systems, their practical experience, and their ability to communicate with machines - the core competency of AI product management.

Why This Question Works#

A prompt isn’t just text - it’s a window into someone’s mental model of AI systems. When someone shares their best prompt, they’re revealing:

  • Their understanding of context hierarchy - How they structure information
  • Their grasp of model limitations - What they account for and avoid
  • Their iteration mindset - Evidence of refinement through testing
  • Their practical experience - Real-world application vs. theoretical knowledge
  • Their communication clarity - How they translate business needs into AI instructions

Unlike hypothetical scenarios, prompts are artifacts of actual work. They can’t be faked or memorized from interview prep guides.

The Evaluation Framework#

Here’s how to assess what you’re seeing:

1. Structure and Clarity (Anthropic Fundamentals)#

Green Flags:

  • Clear, direct instructions without unnecessary fluff2
  • Logical information hierarchy
  • XML tags or markdown for structure (<example>, <context>, <output_format>)3
  • Explicit success criteria defined upfront

Red Flags:

  • Vague or overly creative language
  • No clear structure or organization
  • Missing context or incomplete instructions
  • Trying to be clever instead of clear

2. Model-Specific Knowledge#

This is where it gets interesting. Different models require different approaches:

For Traditional Models (GPT-4, Claude):

  • Uses examples and chain-of-thought reasoning4
  • Includes role assignment (“You are an expert…”)
  • Provides multiple examples to guide behavior
  • Shows understanding of token efficiency

For Reasoning Models (OpenAI o1/o3/o4-mini):

  • Earlier models (o1) avoided chain-of-thought instructions entirely5
  • Newer models (o3/o4-mini) show improved instruction following6
  • Direct, concise problem statements still preferred
  • Less reliance on few-shot examples compared to traditional models
  • Focus on clear problem definition over process guidance

A candidate who shows both approaches demonstrates they understand the fundamental differences between model architectures.

3. Tool Integration Competency#

For Function Calling/Code Interpreter:

  • Clear tool usage boundaries7
  • Explicit instructions about when to use which tool
  • Argument validation requirements
  • Planning and reflection steps built into the workflow8

Advanced Indicators:

  • Evidence of A/B testing different approaches
  • Handling of edge cases and error conditions
  • Integration with RAG systems or external APIs
  • Understanding of context length limitations

4. Real-World Readiness#

Quality Indicators:

  • Handles ambiguous or incomplete user inputs
  • Includes output format specifications
  • Shows evidence of iteration and refinement
  • Demonstrates understanding of business constraints

Experience Markers:

  • References to performance metrics or success criteria
  • Understanding of cost implications (token usage, latency)
  • Consideration of user experience in AI interactions
  • Integration with existing business processes

What to Look For: Green Flags vs Red Flags#

Green Flags ✅#

  • Specific, testable success criteria - “Generate 3 alternative headlines, each under 60 characters”
  • Context boundaries - Clear separation of instructions, examples, and user input
  • Error handling - What to do when inputs are unclear or incomplete
  • Output constraints - Format, length, tone specifications
  • Evidence of testing - “I found this works better when…” or version comments

Red Flags ❌#

  • Generic templates - Obviously copied from online guides
  • Overly complex instructions - Trying to solve everything in one prompt
  • No structure - Wall of text without clear organization
  • Missing context - No explanation of what problem this solves
  • Outdated techniques - Using 2022 prompting advice for 2025 models

Follow-Up Questions to Dig Deeper#

Once you’ve seen their best prompt, these questions reveal even more:

  1. “Walk me through how you developed this prompt.” - Shows their iteration process
  2. “What didn’t work in earlier versions?” - Reveals their testing methodology
  3. “How would you modify this for a different model?” - Tests model-specific knowledge
  4. “What metrics do you use to evaluate prompt performance?” - Shows business thinking
  5. “How do you handle edge cases with this prompt?” - Demonstrates real-world experience

The Meta-Question#

Here’s the beautiful part: asking this question is itself a form of context engineering. You’re:

  • Clearly defining the task (show me your best work)
  • Providing specific success criteria (reveal AI competency)
  • Creating a structured evaluation framework
  • Testing for practical experience over theoretical knowledge

The candidates who recognize this meta-layer - that your interview question is a well-engineered prompt - are the ones who truly understand AI product management.

Beyond the Prompt#

The best candidates won’t just show you a prompt - they’ll tell you the story. They’ll explain the business problem, the iteration process, the metrics they tracked, and the lessons learned. They’ll show you how they think about AI systems as products, not just tools.

Because that’s ultimately what AI product management is about: building bridges between human needs and machine capabilities, one well-crafted prompt at a time.
